## 0. [LED: Improving Masked Diffusion Language Models by Lookahead Editing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23122) <a id="link0"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2610.23122
**Authors:** Yuyan Zhou, Zeyuan Yang, Yuxuan Song, Zhicheng Cai, Hongli Yu, Weiying Ma, Hao Zhou, James Kwok

**Abstract:** arXiv:2610.23122v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) have emerged as a promising alternative to autoregressive language models, enabling non-autoregressive text generation through parallel decoding. Existing dLLMs can be broadly categorized into two families: masked diffusion models (MDMs) and uniform diffusion models (UDMs) In this work, we theoretically show that MDMs are easier to optimize due to their mask-based transition schedule but have lower computational expressivity, whereas UDMs enable iterative state revision and greater expressivity but are substantially harder to train. This trade-off raises a natural question: can we obtain the expressivity of iterative revision without giving up the simple, easy-to-optimize transition schedule of MDMs? Motivated by this analysis, we propose Lookahead Editing (\ours{}), which augments masked diffusion decoding with a rewritable proposal state. At each decoding step, the model performs several lookahead updates to iteratively refine future predictions in this proposal state before committing tokens to the masked sequence. This design introduces a rewritable proposal that uses additional compute to improve expressivity while keeping the simple mask-based transition schedule of MDMs. We fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen3-8B to obtain LED-\texttt{7B} and LED-\texttt{8B}, respectively. Across eight benchmarks, LED SFT yields average gains of $+11.4$ and $+7.7$ points over standard MDM SFT. Moreover, using SFT alone, LED-\texttt{8B} outperforms LLaDA2.1-16B Q-mode, which is trained with continued pre-training, supervised fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 3 (Significant advance in diffusion language models). Proposes Lookahead Editing to improve masked diffusion language models, balancing optimization ease and expressivity, with strong empirical gains on multiple benchmarks.

## 1. [Language Models Learn to Improve Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23344) <a id="link1"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2610.23344
**Authors:** Minhua Lin, Zijun Wang, Zewen Liu, Zhaotian Weng, Bing He, Zhan Shi, Benoit Dumoulin, Xiang Zhang, Suhang Wang, Hanqing Lu

**Abstract:** arXiv:2610.23344v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to automate AI research, including language-model (LM) development, offering a potential path toward recursive self-improvement. However, turning experimental feedback into a learned ability to propose effective improvements across models faces two challenges. First, training such an ability with real feedback is expensive: reinforcement learning (RL) requires evaluating tens of thousands of proposals, each potentially requiring a full model-training run. Even at ten GPU-hours per run, reward collection alone would consume hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours, motivating a cheaper surrogate. Second, surrogate feedback can become unreliable as policy optimization favors overestimated proposals when the proposal distribution shifts. To address these challenges, we propose LM2LM, which trains LLMs with RL to generate effective LM improvement proposals. To provide scalable feedback, we use a research world model (RWM), a frozen LLM that predicts proposal gains from real experimental records provided in context, as the reward model for RL. To maintain feedback reliability, we periodically execute current-policy proposals ranked highly by the RWM and add their measured outcomes to its context. Across eight autoregressive and diffusion LM development environments, real-execution evaluations on held-out tasks show that LM2LM enables Qwen3.8-27B to outperform GPT-6-sol, even when GPT-6-sol is given 300 real experimental records in context, while requiring 33% lower data-generation cost. LM2LM also generalizes to a model family unseen during training.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 1: Proposes a novel RL-based method (LM2LM) to improve language models by learning to propose LM improvements using a research world model as a reward surrogate, addressing challenges in RLHF-like training for instruction-following and model improvement.

## 2. [Tree-Aware Posterior Distillation for Long-Horizon Agentic Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23181) <a id="link2"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2610.23181
**Authors:** Heming Zou, Qi Wang, Yixiu Mao, Yun Qu, Yuyi Guo, Zeyuan Liu, Chenglin Li, Weijie Liu, Saiyong Yang, Xiangyang Ji

**Abstract:** arXiv:2610.23181v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards has become a scalable post-training paradigm for large language models. However, training agents over long-horizon interactions presents two challenges: using only terminal rewards makes turn-level credit assignment sparse and high-variance, while sequential mini-batch updates on a fixed rollout batch introduce policy staleness as the current policy drifts from the behavior policy. Existing approaches obtain denser credit via learned critics, process reward models, or on-policy distillation, but they rely on a value network, step-level annotations, or a stronger external teacher that increase training complexity. Meanwhile, importance sampling and clipping mitigate policy drift, but they do not specify a finite target that stops sequential updates from overshooting. In this work, we introduce TreeOPSD, a critic-free, tree-aware posterior self-distillation framework for long-horizon agentic reinforcement learning. Treating each interaction turn as a tree node, TreeOPSD branches alternative continuations from shared prefixes and recursively backs up terminal rewards into implicit turn-level credit. It then maps each credit to a closed-form, bounded teacher distribution parameterized by the likelihood ratio relative to the frozen behavior policy. Minimizing the divergence between this teacher and the student policy recovers the policy-gradient direction as the tangent at the behavior policy while bounding updates toward a finite target across sequential mini-batches. Empirically, TreeOPSD improves agentic capabilities across several competitive benchmarks and model scales, especially for long-horizon tasks, e.g., raising the average accuracy of Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 by 4.97 points over GRPO on the Deep Research setting.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 1: Introduces TreeOPSD, a new critic-free, tree-aware posterior self-distillation method for long-horizon agentic reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, improving RLHF-like fine-tuning for language models in multi-turn settings.

## 3. [Verbalising Model's Computation for Chain-of-Thought Faithfulness Detection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23275) <a id="link3"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2610.23275
**Authors:** Yujing Wang, Yulan He, Wenya Wang, Hanqi Yan

**Abstract:** arXiv:2610.23275v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Current Chain-of-Thought (CoT) verification methods detect reasoning faithfulness from model outputs or static activations, but are limited in explaining how unfaithfulness develops during generation. We introduce VERITAS to verbalise the model's computation: how different reasoning anchors emerge, transfer across layers, and surface in the output. VERITAS adopts the layer-wise Jacobian projection to produce a verbalised account of the model's internal reasoning evolution at each generated CoT token. By training a classifier on the extracted features along the traces, we show that these structured features provide insightful perspectives on how and where reasoning diverges toward unfaithfulness. Further analysis shows VERITAS can be generalised to different datasets and model architectures. Our work shows that verbalising model computation provides a richer and more interpretable lens on CoT faithfulness, moving beyond detection toward a mechanistic understanding of how reasoning fails.

## 4. [On the SPOT: Enhancing Speculative Decoding for Diffusion Language Models via Commit-Order Prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22543) <a id="link4"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2610.22543
**Authors:** Eliezer Shlomi, Eliron Rahimi, Avi Mendelson, Chaim Baskin

**Abstract:** arXiv:2610.22543v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Diffusion language models (DLMs) promise parallel text generation, but in practice face an unresolved speed-quality trade-off. Exact decoding fills in only one masked position per model pass, while faster multi-token decoding schemes are lossy. Speculative decoding is the standard lossless acceleration for autoregressive models. However, it does not transfer directly to DLMs, which must choose both what to generate and where. We show that this order is the missing piece: the model's own predictions from the previous pass already give the right token at 92-100% of positions, but not which position comes next. Based on this observation, we introduce Speculating Positions Order Trajectory (SPOT), which learns to predict this order. A 0.62M-parameter model reads only the target's output distributions and proposes a tree of candidate continuations, and the target verifies all of them in a single pass by checking whether each equals its own next state. On the verification side, our analysis shows that prior block-diffusion verification approaches either assume $T=0$ or a sequential proposal interface. To support tree-based speculation at any temperature, we propose using coupled-noise match verification for diffusion blocks, following shared-Gumbel-noise coupling approaches. Our comprehensive experiments show that SPOT cuts target passes by up to 6.8x and reaches up to 3.2x the throughput of standard decoding, beating the calibrated and training-free drafting baselines in every task and budget at $T=0$ and $T=1$. In addition, we show that the learned drafter transfers across models, demonstrating successful transfer across six different DLMs. Project page: https://eliezer318.github.io/SPOT-dLLM/

**Comment:** This paper presents a new method (SPOT) to improve decoding speed and quality in diffusion language models, directly matching criterion 3 about advances in diffusion language models for text generation.

## 5. [CoLU: Confidence-Weighted Layerwise Unlearning for Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22721) <a id="link5"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2610.22721
**Authors:** Jingkai Guo, Ziyi Zhao, Chaitali Chakrabarti, Deliang Fan

**Abstract:** arXiv:2610.22721v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Large language model (LLM) unlearning aims to forget certain information while preserving others, without retraining from scratch. SoTA methods such as NPO, SimNPO, and SatImp balance forgetting and retain through whole model fine-tuning, while FOM-UL explores selective layer updates. Prior works show that suppressing target information can damage retained knowledge. This trade-off can vary across layers even under the same unlearning loss. In this work, we propose Confidence-Weighted Layerwise Unlearning (CoLU), which combines a novel confidence-weighted forgetting and retain regularization while updating a single selected transformer layer, rather than the whole model for unlearning. To reduce CoLU compute cost, we further propose Brief Layer Screening (BLS), which uses a newly defined normalized forgetting-retention (NFR) score to first rank layers through a brief unlearning trials using a subset of forgetting dataset and fewer epochs. BLS pre-selects top-k ranked layer candidates, which will then be fully profiled using full data analysis. BLS is proven to reduce the runtime of CoLU by 60.2-79.7% with almost the same performance. On TOFU dataset, CoLU improves both forgetting and retention over NPO, SatImp, and GA-based FOM-UL in all six TOFU settings, and over SimNPO in five of the six settings. On MUSE Books and News datasets, CoLU achieves the highest retained KnowMem and the PrivLeak closest to zero among the compared SOTA unlearning methods. CoLU presents a new record performance in both unlearning and retain in most settings up to date. Moreover, across the measured settings, average CoLU GPU memory usage is 77.4% to 79.5% lower than full-model unlearning.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 9: Presents new methods for machine unlearning techniques specifically designed for large language models, introducing Confidence-Weighted Layerwise Unlearning (CoLU) and Brief Layer Screening (BLS) to improve forgetting and retention trade-offs.

## Paper selection prompt
 1. New methodological improvements to RLHF or instruction-following which are specific fine-tuning steps that are taken to make language models better at following user instructions across a range of tasks.
    - Relevant: papers that discuss specific methods like RLHF, or instruction-tuning datasets, improving these methods, or analyzing them. Usually these papers will explicitly mention RLHF, instruction-following or instruction-tuning.
    - Not relevant: papers about adaptation to some task. Simply following instructions or inputs are not sufficient.
 2. Shows new powerful test set contamination or membership inference methods for language models. Test set contamination is the phenomenon where a language model observes a benchmark dataset during pretraining.
    - Relevant: test statistics that can detect contamination of benchmarks in language models. statistics that can provide guarantees are more interesting. membership inference methods that are general enough to apply to language models are also relevant.
    - Not relevant: any papers that do not consider language models, or that do not consider test set contamination.
 3. Shows a significant advance in the performance of diffusion language models.
    - Relevant: papers that study language models that are also diffusion models. Continuous diffusions are even more relevant, while discrete diffusions are less so.
    - Not relevant: papers about image diffusions like DALL-E or Stable Diffusion, or papers that do not explicitly mention language models or applications to text.
 4. Describes new paradigms to evaluating open-ended text generation. Evaluating the outputs of language models is hard, especially in open-ended settings like for chatbots.
    - Relevant: papers that fundamentally rethink language model evaluation -- especially by accounting for subjectivity or using adversaries.
    - Not relevant: specific evaluations for specific tasks, identifying new properties or flaws of language models, or simply collecting new data.
 5. Conducts surveys or provides data into real-world usage and safety properties of language models.
    - Relevant: papers that create new datasets or surveys on real-world usage of language models.
    - Not relevant: papers that apply language models to new real-world tasks.
 6. Studies 'scaling laws' in the context of neural networks. Scaling laws refer to the very clear power-law relationship between the size or computational power used to train a model and the performance of that model.
    - Relevant: theoretical or conceptual explanation behind scaling laws for language models.
    - Not relevant: papers that have experiments at different model scales (but do not explicitly fit a scaling law) or papers that mention scaling laws, but the scaling laws are not the central subject of the paper
 7. Presents new methods or improvements for sparse autoencoders in neural network interpretability. Sparse autoencoders are unsupervised learning techniques that learn sparse representations of neural network activations to understand what features networks detect.
    - Relevant: papers that explicitly mention sparse autoencoders, sparse coding, or sparse feature extraction applied to understanding neural networks, language models, or other AI systems.
    - Not relevant: papers about general autoencoders without sparsity constraints, or sparse methods not applied to interpretability.
 8. Demonstrates new mechanistic interpretability techniques or discoveries about how neural networks work internally. Mechanistic interpretability seeks to understand the algorithms and circuits that neural networks learn.
    - Relevant: papers about circuit analysis, feature visualization, activation patching, causal interventions on neural networks, or reverse-engineering specific algorithms learned by models.
    - Not relevant: papers that only do black-box analysis or correlation studies without mechanistic understanding.
 9. Shows advances in machine unlearning techniques for removing specific knowledge or behaviors from trained models.
    - Relevant: papers on forgetting specific data, removing harmful capabilities, or selectively editing model knowledge while preserving other capabilities.
    - Not relevant: papers about general model editing or fine-tuning without specific unlearning focus.
 10. Presents methods for tamper resistance, robustness against adversarial modifications, or secure AI systems.
    - Relevant: papers on preventing model poisoning, backdoor attacks, or making AI systems resistant to tampering or manipulation.
    - Not relevant: papers only about general adversarial robustness without security/tamper resistance focus.
 11. Studies AI alignment techniques, value learning, or methods to ensure AI systems behave according to human values and intentions.
    - Relevant: papers on reward modeling, constitutional AI, value alignment, or techniques to make AI systems more aligned with human preferences.
    - Not relevant: papers focused only on performance improvement without alignment considerations.
 12. Advances in LLM reasoning capabilities, chain-of-thought reasoning, or understanding how language models perform complex reasoning tasks.
    - Relevant: papers on step-by-step reasoning, logical inference in LLMs, reasoning chain analysis, or methods to improve reasoning capabilities.
    - Not relevant: papers about reasoning in non-LLM contexts or that don't focus on reasoning mechanisms.
 13. Presents new methods for AI control, oversight, or monitoring of AI system behavior during deployment.
    - Relevant: papers on AI governance techniques, monitoring systems, control mechanisms, or methods to maintain oversight of deployed AI systems.
    - Not relevant: papers focused only on training-time controls without deployment considerations.
 14. Shows advances in chain-of-thought monitoring, reasoning transparency, or methods to understand AI decision-making processes.
    - Relevant: papers on monitoring reasoning chains, making AI reasoning more transparent, or techniques to track how models arrive at conclusions.
    - Not relevant: papers about general explainability without specific focus on reasoning processes.
 15. Studies ControlArena, Inspect framework, or similar evaluation platforms for AI safety and control.
    - Relevant: papers using or extending these evaluation frameworks, comparative studies using these platforms, or methodological improvements to these tools.
    - Not relevant: papers that mention these tools only in passing without substantial use or analysis.
 16. Advances in latent reasoning, implicit reasoning processes, or understanding hidden computational processes in neural networks.
    - Relevant: papers on uncovering implicit reasoning, analyzing hidden computational steps, or methods to make latent reasoning processes more explicit.
    - Not relevant: papers about explicit reasoning without focus on hidden or latent processes.
 17. Studies data poisoning attacks or defenses in machine learning systems.
    - Relevant: papers on poisoning training data to manipulate model behavior, detecting poisoned data, or defending against data poisoning attacks.
    - Not relevant: papers about general adversarial examples at inference time without training data manipulation.
 18. Advances in data-centric AI approaches that focus on improving data quality, curation, or data-driven methods for ML.
    - Relevant: papers on data quality assessment, data selection, data augmentation strategies, curriculum learning based on data properties, or systematic approaches to dataset creation and curation.
    - Not relevant: papers that simply use standard datasets without focusing on data-centric methodology.
 19. Studies security of LLMs and methods to protect language models from attacks or exploitation.
    - Relevant: papers on prompt injection defenses, jailbreak prevention, securing LLM deployments, protecting against extraction attacks, or hardening LLM systems against adversarial users.
    - Not relevant: papers about using LLMs for security applications, or general robustness without security focus.
