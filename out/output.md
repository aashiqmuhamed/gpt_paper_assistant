## 0. [Beyond the Log-Linear Frontier: Pre-Asymptotic Analysis of Finite-Budget Neural Scaling Laws](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13534) <a id="link0"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.13534
**Authors:** Tianyu Zhao, Zhixiang Ren

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.13534v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Classical neural scaling laws model excess risk as a separable power law in model capacity $N$ and dataset size $D$. At finite budgets, however, empirical learning curves and compute-optimal allocations depart from this log-linear frontier. This paper analyzes how spectral and domain heterogeneity govern these finite-scale departures. Along the capacity axis, Mellin--Barnes analysis of covariance resolvents yields a three-term expansion where competing spectral and regularization modes produce finite-scale curvature and logarithmic transitions. Because fitting multiple power laws on narrow pilot grids is ill-conditioned, we eliminate the loss floor via first-order Aitken differencing to obtain a well-conditioned closed-form initializer. Along the data axis, KKT duality reduces $K$-domain mixing to scalar water-filling, yielding finite-budget bottleneck bounds, an online sampling curriculum, and a simplex Hessian metric for cross-domain transfer. Along the iso-compute frontier $C=6ND$, we prove that the optimal allocation exponent drifts in proportion to capacity and data exponent variances, while Schur-complement reduction separates intrinsic drift from frozen-hyperparameter bias. Across 45 operator spectra, Pythia (70M--12B), Chinchilla (245 runs), and RegMix (17 domains), our estimators achieve $5.89\%$ holdout MAPE, cut high-compute allocation regret by up to $76.4\%$, and reduce cross-scale mixture RMSE by $59.4\%$ with zero selection regret.

## 1. [Are Dense Rewards Worth Their Tokens in GRPO?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13560) <a id="link1"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.13560
**Authors:** Vikash Singh, Weicong Chen, Debargha Ganguly, Yanyan Zhang, Mohsen Hariri, Sreehari Sankar, Wang Yang, Chaoda Song, Alexander Nemecek, Osama Zafar, Yuting Shao, Kai Ye, Biyao Zhang, Xinpeng Li, Shouren Wang, Erman Ayday, Jing Ma, Sanmukh Rao Kuppannagari, Vipin Chaudhary

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.13560v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: RLVR trains language models to reason by rewarding correct final answers. A widely used algorithm, group-relative policy optimization (GRPO), gives every token of a solution the same credit, so dense process rewards have been proposed to credit intermediate steps, for example with a Monte-Carlo reward that scores each step by sampling continuations from it. Comparing such a reward with GRPO per training update, or vetting it with a gradient test on a single batch, leaves out what it costs: in our setting, the continuations alone take 91% of all generated tokens. We introduce a matched-compute protocol that gives every training intervention the same budget, measured in generated tokens and training GPU-hours, and tests it in preregistered studies with controls for side effects. Across 1,668 runs on six models, matching compute reverses the ranking. On Qwen2.5-1.5B, the Monte-Carlo reward leads standard GRPO per update, yet ends 14.3 percentage points lower at equal tokens and 7.8 points lower at equal training time, and none of four dense-reward variants outperforms GRPO even with as much training time. A per-token gradient test orders these rewards as training does but overestimates each by one to two points, as much as the gains it is meant to detect. In contrast, training on the prompts whose predicted success is nearest 1/2 improves accuracy at matched compute, by 1.7 points on Qwen3-0.6B and 0.8 on Qwen2.5-1.5B. These gains replicate on fresh seeds and unseen problems, and a simple rule, that the candidate prompts are predicted to fail more often than not, predicted where they appear in five of six preregistered tests. Both gains come early in training, and standard GRPO with twice the initial learning rate comes within half a point of them. We argue that training interventions should be validated at matched compute, which overturns some apparent gains and confirms others.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 1: discusses specific fine-tuning steps and reward methods (GRPO, dense rewards) for improving instruction-following and reasoning in language models, with a novel matched-compute protocol to fairly evaluate training interventions.

## 2. [A Theory of Policy Optimization in Post-Training: Coverage Collapse and Reference Resets](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14467) <a id="link2"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.14467
**Authors:** Audrey Huang, Nan Jiang, Sham M. Kakade, Sadhika Malladi, Akshay Krishnamurthy, Dylan J. Foster

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.14467v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Policy optimization is central to language model post-training, yet principled guidance remains limited. We revisit its theoretical foundations to identify algorithms that learn near-optimal policies using total samples and gradient steps governed by the base model's response coverage, a measure of how readily it generates optimal responses. We call such algorithms coverage-preserving. This guarantee captures the goal of amplifying capabilities accessible through sampling from the base model.   (1) Coverage collapse. REINFORCE, the basis for PPO and GRPO, can drive the probability of optimal responses to near zero, even as reward improves. This coverage collapse stalls optimization, and convergence can require exponentially many steps despite good initial coverage. The failure arises even with exact gradients and persists under KL regularization to the base model.   (2) Reference resets. We show that repeatedly resetting the KL reference to the most recent policy mitigates coverage collapse. We formalize this intervention in Approximate Policy Mirror Descent (APMD), a meta-algorithm that approximately solves a sequence of KL-regularized RL subproblems. APMD enables stronger regularization, making each subproblem easier to solve, while reference resets allow convergence to global optimality.   (3) Coverage-preserving algorithms. For softmax linear policies over pretrained features, we use APMD to establish the first global convergence guarantees showing that widely used families of post-training algorithms are coverage-preserving. These include stylized PPO and GRPO variants with explicit KL regularization and reference resets, and squared-loss algorithms that implement them implicitly, including those used to train the Kimi models.   These results clarify how regularization, clipping, and objective choice affect the sampling and optimization costs of post-training.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 1 closely: provides new theoretical insights and algorithmic improvements for policy optimization in post-training of language models, including coverage collapse and reference resets relevant to RLHF and instruction-following fine-tuning.

## 3. [Rethinking in Spikes: Mitigating Hallucinations in MDLMs with Step-Aware Decoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12901) <a id="link3"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.12901
**Authors:** Zhongxing Xu, Zhonghua Wang, Zhe Qian, Shiyan Su, Ming Hu, Xiaocheng Zou, Wei Feng, Mingquan Lin, Yuyin Zhou, Yifan Peng, Hamid Rezatofighi, Zongyuan Ge

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.12901v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Recent advancements in multimodal diffusion language models (MDLMs) have exhibited strong global modeling capabilities through iterative refinement. We observe that mask uncertainty follows an overall downward trend during iterative denoising, while a few denoising steps exhibit localized entropy spikes closely associated with hallucinations. We argue that anomalous steps can be identified by measuring the entropy deviation of each denoising step from its neighboring temporal window. We hypothesize that discrete token commitment discards the distributional information at masked positions, limiting the continued interaction between candidate semantics and the context during entropy spikes. With this goal, we present StepRefine, a plug-and-play decoding strategy that leverages semantic context to achieve reliable denoising. The core of our method lies in a step-aware switch between discrete denoising and latent refinement. The model employs probability-weighted embeddings at entropy spike steps to preserve diverse reasoning hypotheses and performs multiple rounds of continuous refinement. Meanwhile, a refinement controller monitors distributional stability to reduce unnecessary loops and switch the model back to standard discrete denoising. Through extensive experiments, StepRefine demonstrates significant hallucination mitigation across different MDLMs on multimodal benchmarks.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 3: Presents a significant advance in diffusion language models by proposing StepRefine, a step-aware decoding strategy that mitigates hallucinations in multimodal diffusion language models through a novel switch between discrete denoising and latent refinement.

## 4. [Blurring the Block Boundaries: Overlapping Block Denoising for Efficient Diffusion Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14387) <a id="link4"></a>

**Authors:** Amir Mann, Jonathan Fhima, Shaked Brody, Or Avitan, Asif Zamir, Shahar Tsiper, Niv Nayman

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.14387v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Diffusion Language Models (DLMs) have emerged as effective alternatives to autoregressive models for latency-critical tasks, enabling faster token generation. Recent advances employ block diffusion with self-editing capabilities, where each block refines tokens iteratively before advancing to the next one. Yet each block is only as fast as its slowest token, and the next block cannot start before it. We suggest Blurring the Block Boundaries (B3), a novel method with which two blocks are refined in parallel within a shared forward pass. B3 calibrates itself on the fly, opening the next block only once the model is ready to start generating it. Evaluated on state-of-the-art DLMs across 10 diverse datasets spanning varied tasks and difficulty levels, B3 results in on par generation quality and 11.5-14.5% speedup compared to the base model, pushing the Pareto frontier for latency-quality trade-offs in DLM inference.

## 5. [Linear Probes to Bayesian Manifolds: Geometry of Arithmetic in Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12998) <a id="link5"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.12998
**Authors:** Anshul Kumar, Neha Prasad, Manoj Kumar, Barnab\'as P\'oczos, Deeksha Varshney

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.12998v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Linear probes are a standard method to study geometric structures, such as helices and circles, from the activations of large language models (LLMs), and such structure is often taken as evidence that the model computes with it. Probe accuracy alone cannot establish this, because it conflates four separate claims. These are (1) that the concept is decodable from the activations, (2) that they form a specific shape, (3) that the shape belongs to the probed concept rather than to a related concept the model also represents, and (4) that the output of the model depends on the shape. We call these claims decodability, shape, ownership, and causal use, and we test them separately with a four-stage Bayesian pipeline. Stage 2 compares candidate shapes and selects the best one only when its evidence passes a predefined threshold. Stage 3 removes a set of related concepts, specified in advance, and tests whether the shape is owned or inherited. Stage 4 intervenes on the shape to test whether the output of the model depends on it. We apply the pipeline to two-digit addition and multiplication in GPT-J 6B, Llama 3.1 8B, and Pythia 6.9B. Nearly every concept is decodable, but the decoded directions overlap substantially across concepts. Every strongly supported comparison selects a ribbon, which is a helix whose radius changes with the value, or a torus. Of 1,330 cells with an ownership verdict, 19% of shapes are owned and 81% are inherited. Owned and inherited shapes move the output about equally often. Intervening on the shape retains approximately 61% of the effect of intervening on the full probe while using at most five dimensions, against a median of nine for the probe. Our results indicate that a probe verdict alone overstates what a model computes with, and we propose that probe results be reported as verdicts on all four claims rather than as a single accuracy.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 8: Demonstrates a new mechanistic interpretability technique with a four-stage Bayesian pipeline to disentangle decodability, shape, ownership, and causal use of geometric structures in LLM activations.

## Paper selection prompt
 1. New methodological improvements to RLHF or instruction-following which are specific fine-tuning steps that are taken to make language models better at following user instructions across a range of tasks.
    - Relevant: papers that discuss specific methods like RLHF, or instruction-tuning datasets, improving these methods, or analyzing them. Usually these papers will explicitly mention RLHF, instruction-following or instruction-tuning.
    - Not relevant: papers about adaptation to some task. Simply following instructions or inputs are not sufficient.
 2. Shows new powerful test set contamination or membership inference methods for language models. Test set contamination is the phenomenon where a language model observes a benchmark dataset during pretraining.
    - Relevant: test statistics that can detect contamination of benchmarks in language models. statistics that can provide guarantees are more interesting. membership inference methods that are general enough to apply to language models are also relevant.
    - Not relevant: any papers that do not consider language models, or that do not consider test set contamination.
 3. Shows a significant advance in the performance of diffusion language models.
    - Relevant: papers that study language models that are also diffusion models. Continuous diffusions are even more relevant, while discrete diffusions are less so.
    - Not relevant: papers about image diffusions like DALL-E or Stable Diffusion, or papers that do not explicitly mention language models or applications to text.
 4. Describes new paradigms to evaluating open-ended text generation. Evaluating the outputs of language models is hard, especially in open-ended settings like for chatbots.
    - Relevant: papers that fundamentally rethink language model evaluation -- especially by accounting for subjectivity or using adversaries.
    - Not relevant: specific evaluations for specific tasks, identifying new properties or flaws of language models, or simply collecting new data.
 5. Conducts surveys or provides data into real-world usage and safety properties of language models.
    - Relevant: papers that create new datasets or surveys on real-world usage of language models.
    - Not relevant: papers that apply language models to new real-world tasks.
 6. Studies 'scaling laws' in the context of neural networks. Scaling laws refer to the very clear power-law relationship between the size or computational power used to train a model and the performance of that model.
    - Relevant: theoretical or conceptual explanation behind scaling laws for language models.
    - Not relevant: papers that have experiments at different model scales (but do not explicitly fit a scaling law) or papers that mention scaling laws, but the scaling laws are not the central subject of the paper
 7. Presents new methods or improvements for sparse autoencoders in neural network interpretability. Sparse autoencoders are unsupervised learning techniques that learn sparse representations of neural network activations to understand what features networks detect.
    - Relevant: papers that explicitly mention sparse autoencoders, sparse coding, or sparse feature extraction applied to understanding neural networks, language models, or other AI systems.
    - Not relevant: papers about general autoencoders without sparsity constraints, or sparse methods not applied to interpretability.
 8. Demonstrates new mechanistic interpretability techniques or discoveries about how neural networks work internally. Mechanistic interpretability seeks to understand the algorithms and circuits that neural networks learn.
    - Relevant: papers about circuit analysis, feature visualization, activation patching, causal interventions on neural networks, or reverse-engineering specific algorithms learned by models.
    - Not relevant: papers that only do black-box analysis or correlation studies without mechanistic understanding.
 9. Shows advances in machine unlearning techniques for removing specific knowledge or behaviors from trained models.
    - Relevant: papers on forgetting specific data, removing harmful capabilities, or selectively editing model knowledge while preserving other capabilities.
    - Not relevant: papers about general model editing or fine-tuning without specific unlearning focus.
 10. Presents methods for tamper resistance, robustness against adversarial modifications, or secure AI systems.
    - Relevant: papers on preventing model poisoning, backdoor attacks, or making AI systems resistant to tampering or manipulation.
    - Not relevant: papers only about general adversarial robustness without security/tamper resistance focus.
 11. Studies AI alignment techniques, value learning, or methods to ensure AI systems behave according to human values and intentions.
    - Relevant: papers on reward modeling, constitutional AI, value alignment, or techniques to make AI systems more aligned with human preferences.
    - Not relevant: papers focused only on performance improvement without alignment considerations.

    - Relevant: papers on step-by-step reasoning, logical inference in LLMs, reasoning chain analysis, or methods to improve reasoning capabilities.
    - Not relevant: papers about reasoning in non-LLM contexts or that don't focus on reasoning mechanisms.
 13. Presents new methods for AI control, oversight, or monitoring of AI system behavior during deployment.
