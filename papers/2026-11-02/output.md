## 0. [Asynchronous Flow Map Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42018) <a id="link0"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2610.42018
**Authors:** Chanhyuk Lee, Wonjung Kim, Patrick Pynadath, Jaehoon Yoo, Satoshi Hayakawa, Ruqi Zhang, Jinwoo Kim, Seunghoon Hong

**Abstract:** arXiv:2610.42018v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: The order in which tokens are predicted in language modeling has a profound impact on the quality of generated sequences. Autoregressive and masked diffusion models iteratively fill in tokens and can exploit such orderings across generation steps, but how to leverage an ordering for one-step parallel language modeling is unclear. We introduce asynchronous flow map language models (aFMLMs), which incorporate prediction order into one-step modeling by learning the flow maps of asynchronous flows. These flows specify an ordering through a tokenwise time schedule that controls the relative denoising progress of tokens. We formulate a general class of asynchronous flows that admit both prescribed and learned, state-adaptive schedules, show that they define valid flow maps, and develop new methods for their flow map distillation. The resulting models can generate all tokens in a single forward pass by internalizing the ordering encoded in the flow into their hidden computations. On Sudoku solving and code generation for GSM8K math word problems, aFMLM establishes new state-of-the-art one-step performance.

**Comment:** This paper introduces asynchronous flow map language models (aFMLMs), a new diffusion-based language modeling paradigm that incorporates token prediction order into one-step parallel generation via learned asynchronous flows. It explicitly studies diffusion language models applied to text and shows state-of-the-art results on code generation and math problems. ## 1. [What Should LLMs Forget: Reinforcement Unlearning of Knowledge Associations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41616) <a id="link1"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2610.41616
**Authors:** Wutong Xu, Yun Qu, Haiyan Feng, Qi Wang, Xiangyang Ji

**Abstract:** arXiv:2610.41616v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Large language models (LLMs) acquire extensive factual knowledge during pre-training, which may include sensitive, private, or copyrighted information, motivating the development of unlearning methods that selectively remove specific knowledge while preserving general capabilities. However, existing approaches typically optimize entire responses without explicitly identifying the knowledge to be forgotten, resulting in coarse and uncontrolled forgetting. To address this, we revisit the forgetting target of LLM unlearning and introduce Knowledge Association (KA), which characterizes the association between a query context and its target knowledge and is instantiated by the corresponding conditional predictive distribution, as the fundamental target of forgetting. Based on KA, we propose Knowledge Association Reinforcement Unlearning (KARU), a unified reinforcement learning framework that suppresses forgotten knowledge associations while preserving retained ones. Specifically, KARU derives complementary mechanisms from the KA-based formulation: token-level reward construction for finer-grained forgetting supervision, distribution-level regularization for precise knowledge suppression, and proximity-aware retain sampling to mitigate collateral degradation. Experiments across diverse LLMs on representative benchmarks demonstrate that KARU consistently improves forgetting effectiveness over existing baselines while maintaining comparable retention performance and general utility.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 9: Presents a new reinforcement learning based unlearning method (KARU) to selectively forget specific knowledge associations in LLMs while preserving other capabilities. This is a clear advance in machine unlearning techniques for language models.

## 2. [CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42054) <a id="link2"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2610.42054
**Authors:** Xiao Yu, Justin Chih-Yao Chen, Zaid Khan, Hyunji Lee, Mohit Bansal

**Abstract:** arXiv:2610.42054v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become the standard method for training language models to reason. However, RLVR typically relies solely on outcome correctness, which does not guarantee that the CoT causally contributes to the final answer. Without this connection, outcome rewards may fail to reinforce high-quality reasoning. Existing causality metrics assess CoT causality by perturbing the CoT, such as deleting reasoning steps or injecting errors, and measuring changes in the final answer. However, these perturbations inevitably alter information in each step. Consequently, high causality scores may reflect sensitivity to information changes introduced by perturbation rather than true causality. Such confounding effects further induce undesirable reasoning behaviors when these metrics are used as training rewards, including generating less informative CoTs and reducing exploration and self-correction. To address this problem, we propose CRoS (Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs), a novel CoT causality metric that perturbs the order of reasoning steps without altering content, thereby preserving information within each step. On Reasoning Gym with Gemma-3-4B, CRoS outperforms existing causality and outcome rewards in out-of-domain accuracy, CoT quality, and CoT faithfulness. Specifically, CRoS achieves 46.8% accuracy on five unseen logic and games datasets, outperforming the strongest baseline (43.8%). It also achieves better CoT quality, consistently outperforming baselines in CoT-Pass@K from K=1 to 128, averaging 43.4% vs. 41.4%. For CoT faithfulness, CRoS achieves a hint acknowledgment score of 34.0% vs. 20.2%.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 1: Proposes a novel causality reward metric (CRoS) for chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning that improves instruction-following and reasoning faithfulness in language models by better aligning rewards with causal contribution of reasoning steps, a methodological improvement relevant to instruction-following and RLHF-like training.

## 5. [On the Origin and Evolution of Training-Inference Mismatch in LLM Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40945) <a id="link5"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2610.40945
**Authors:** Yu Li, Yufa Zhou, Tian Lan

**Abstract:** arXiv:2610.40945v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: On-policy reinforcement learning for large language models assumes that training responses are sampled from the current policy. In practice, an inference engine samples the responses and a separate trainer computes the gradients, and the \textbf{training-inference mismatch} between the two makes the training data off-policy. The mismatch has two sources, the numerics of the engine and the stale weights of asynchronous sampling, and a correction that stabilizes training under one source can fail under the other. \textit{We ask which property of a mismatch source decides the correction it needs.} Evaluating the trainer at the sampler's weights splits each sampled-token error exactly into a \textit{numerical discrepancy} of the engine and a \textit{policy lag} of the trainer, and we follow both through training. The numerical discrepancy stays approximately constant, so it biases the updates by a nearly fixed amount, and centering the score at each prefix removes its leading bias. The policy lag is produced by the trainer's own updates: the bias it leaves enters the next update, which enlarges the next lag and closes a feedback loop. Importance weighting cuts this loop only while the lag ratio stays concentrated, and on Qwen3-1.7B it fails at the step where the effective sample size of the ratio falls below one half. From this account we derive the \textbf{discrepancy-lag correction} (DLC), which centers the numerical discrepancy, clips the lag ratio against the sampler's weights and resynchronizes the sampler when this effective sample size falls below one half. Under a stale sampler, DLC keeps the KL divergence from on-policy training within twice the KL between two independent on-policy trainings on Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-1.7B, and its resynchronization prevents collapses that the per-token corrections leave and that a KL measured on the sampler's prefixes does not detect.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 1 closely: proposes a new correction method (discrepancy-lag correction) addressing training-inference mismatch in RLHF/on-policy RL for LLMs, a specific fine-tuning step improving instruction-following training stability.

## 6. [From Representation to Readout: Mechanistic Analysis of Membership Signals in Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41445) <a id="link6"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2610.41445
**Authors:** Wenxi Dai, Jingyu Hu, Yanting Yang, Mengnan Du

**Abstract:** arXiv:2610.41445v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Membership inference aims to determine whether a particular example was included in a model's training data. We introduce Decodable Information Score, Used Information Score, and Readout Accessibility Gap (DUG), a set of complementary measures that separately quantify membership information decodable from internal representations and the extent to which it is exposed through the model's readout. ## 8. [Where, When, and How Many Bits? Scaling Laws for SGD with Stochastic Rounding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41074) <a id="link8"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2610.41074
**Authors:** Zhichao Wang, Michael W. Mahoney

**Abstract:** arXiv:2610.41074v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Low-precision training reduces computation but introduces additional rounding error. We theoretically study how stochastic rounding (SR) affects prediction risk as model size, training time, and precision change. We consider a two-layer linear network with a fixed random first layer, trained by mini-batch SGD on fresh Gaussian data with power-law spectra. At each SGD step, the features used in forward and backward computations are rounded independently. We prove matched upper and lower bounds on prediction error for the final model and for uniform parameter averaging, with general learning-rate and precision schedules chosen before training. At a fixed compute budget, lower precision can improve prediction when the benefit of a larger model or longer training outweighs the additional SR-induced error. Model simulations illustrate these tradeoffs, and experiments with an MLP and a small transformer examine rounding choices beyond the theoretical model.

## 59. [When Should AI Agents Ask? Clarification Requests Under Limited Human Attention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41129) <a id="link59"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2610.41129
**Authors:** Hanzhao Wang, Shengjie Liu, Zhengchao Wang

**Abstract:** arXiv:2610.41129v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: AI agents increasingly carry out delegated tasks in parallel, with one person overseeing several of them at once. In practice, an agent often lacks information its task requires, such as an unstated preference, constraint, or specification. It can ask the human to clarify, or it can search for the information itself, for example in past records, which takes time and effort and may fail. In this paper, we study how to coordinate these decisions across several agents that share one human with limited attention. We build a stylized model in which each agent can search, ask, or continue the parts of its task that do not depend on the missing information, and we formulate the problem as a dynamic program. We first show that whether an agent should ask depends on more than its own uncertainty: an agent with other work to do can be better off asking earlier, and which question the human should answer first depends on all waiting questions, so no ranking computed agent by agent is optimal. We then propose the Pairwise Coordination Policy (PCP), which uses exact one- and two-task problems to estimate how pairs of tasks delay each other when they share the human. Theoretically, PCP is exactly optimal when at most two requirements remain unresolved and costs at most twice the optimum when every unresolved requirement must be resolved by the human. We evaluate PCP on 864 synthetic cases and in a case study with LLM agents. On the synthetic benchmark, PCP's mean gap to optimal cost is 0.07%, and it reduces cost by an average of 18-31% relative to three standard rules for when to ask. In the LLM case study, agents completed every task correctly, and PCP achieved the lowest mean cost in all tested settings.
