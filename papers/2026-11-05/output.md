## 0. [LLMs Don't Know What They Know: Benchmarking Temporal Self-Knowledge for Financial Backtesting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06808) <a id="link0"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.06808
**Authors:** Lingfei Qian, Yan Wang, Xueqing Peng, Yupeng Cao, Yuechen Jiang, Zhuohan Xie, Anke Xu, Wenbo Cao, Jiarui Chen, Zehan Li, Jimin Huang, Alejandro Lopez-Lira, Yuehua Tang, Sophia Ananiadou, Jian-Yun Nie

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.06808v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Financial backtests of large language models (LLMs) are meaningful only if the model did not already know the outcomes it is asked to predict. Practitioners guard against this by trusting a vendor's stated knowledge cutoff, by asking the model what it knows, or by instructing it to answer as of a past date. To verify whether any of these safeguards can be trusted, we introduce FinDendro, a benchmark of 2,226 dated U.S. facts from 1999 to 2026, covering monthly macroeconomic series, month-end stock prices and Federal Reserve decisions. Each fact yields four prompts: a value question, Recall and Predict questions that pair each candidate value with a claim of knowing or guessing without a declared date, and a Predict question with a historical declared date. Controls for persistence and dominant outcomes keep chance accuracy near 0.27. Results show that for most frontier models, self-reports understate knowledge. GPT-5 answers 76% of controlled facts correctly but claims to know 38%, and answers 61% of the facts it says it does not know. Knowledge claims can persist beyond the measured knowledge boundary, by up to seven quarters, and a declared date removes most of the claims of prior knowledge while leaving accuracy unchanged. Backtests inside a model's training window therefore cannot be certified leak-free by self-report, instruction or stated cutoff. These findings motivate behavioral measurement of temporal knowledge boundaries, and FinDendro provides a benchmark and procedure for selecting backtest periods based on demonstrated factual knowledge.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 2: Introduces FinDendro, a benchmark and behavioral measurement method for detecting temporal knowledge contamination and self-knowledge in LLMs, revealing test set contamination issues and limits of self-reporting knowledge cutoffs. Provides a novel statistical approach to contamination detection in language models.

## 1. [Unifying and Beyond the Theory of Pervasive Capability Degradation in LLM Post-training SFT from Feature Interference](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05003) <a id="link1"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.05003
**Authors:** Hang Chen, Jiaying Zhu, Wenya Wang

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.05003v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Pervasive Capability Degradation (PCD) refers to the phenomenon in which supervised fine-tuning (SFT), while adapting a large language model (LLM) to a target task, often erodes the general capabilities acquired during pre-training. Prevailing explanations attribute PCD to macroscopic distribution shift and therefore lack microscopic manipulability, leaving existing mitigation methods without a complete theoretical foundation. In this paper, we first develop a bottom-up geometric theory of PCD grounded in feature interference under superposition: narrow-domain SFT data disrupt the almost-orthogonal feature structure formed during pre-training, and the resulting interference drives activations across decision hyperplanes in the output Laguerre-Voronoi geometry, flipping predictions on general tasks. This theory unifies existing methods into six paradigms and reveals both the source of their efficacy and their theoretical limits. We then prove that cross-entropy under one-hot supervision cannot resolve PCD on its own, and accordingly propose the AEGIS loss, which directly constrains the drift of activation margins along all competing token directions, thereby decoupling interference suppression from task adaptation. Across 3 model families and 14 SFT tasks, AEGIS outperforms representative methods of every paradigm on target tasks and pervasive capabilities simultaneously, at only about 1.3 times the computational cost of standard SFT. Its generality, scalability, and mechanistic interpretability offer a new perspective on fully resolving PCD.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 1: Proposes a new theoretical framework and loss (AEGIS) to mitigate Pervasive Capability Degradation in supervised fine-tuning of LLMs, which is a fine-tuning step to improve instruction-following and general capabilities. Provides mechanistic interpretability and a novel loss function for better instruction tuning.

## 2. [Forget What Comes Next: LLM Unlearning via Statistical Independence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03955) <a id="link2"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.03955
**Authors:** Andrii Shkabrii, Claudia Plant, Martin Pawelczyk

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.03955v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Large language models (LLMs) can memorize and reproduce fragments of their training data, raising privacy and legal concerns that motivate machine unlearning. Yet poisoned training examples can remain statistically detectable after unlearning, which raises the question of whether current unlearning objectives remove what the model has memorized at all. In this work, we introduce PINT, a novel algorithm for machine unlearning that uses the Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC) to reduce the statistical dependence between tokens to be forgotten and the internal representations used to predict them. We formally relate our HSIC-based objective to Exact Memorization through a computable upper bound, connecting the training objective directly to the model's ability to reproduce the forgotten tokens. In extensive experiments, we compare forgetting-utility trade-offs across eight settings with models up to 7B parameters and demonstrate scalability to 70B parameter models. Our evaluations cover unlearning Gaussian poisons, personal information, hazardous knowledge in biology and cybersecurity, and copyrighted books and news articles. PINT achieves the strongest forgetting-utility trade-off on average and is the only method to make Gaussian poisons undetectable while maintaining near-original model utility. Code is available at: https://github.com/shkabrii/PINT

**Comment:** Matches criterion 9: proposes a new machine unlearning method (PINT) for LLMs using statistical independence (HSIC) to remove memorized data, with formal connections to exact memorization and strong empirical results.

## 3. [D3D: Decoupled Diffusion Drafting for Asynchronous Speculative Decoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06425) <a id="link3"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.06425
**Authors:** Baoyou Chen, Ruiqiao Mei, Guancong Lin, Tao Song, Weihao Yuan, Qun Liu, Siyu Zhu

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.06425v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Target-conditioned diffusion drafters predict candidate tokens in parallel, yet each new block must wait for updated target-model hidden states. This forces drafting and verification to run sequentially, limiting speedup even though each block is generated in one pass. We introduce D3D, a standalone diffusion drafter that removes this inter-round dependency. D3D combines blockwise denoising with auxiliary autoregressive supervision to achieve long accepted prefixes without target hidden states. This independence lets D3D draft successor blocks during verification in one packed pass, enabling asynchronous speculative decoding. We progressively prune layers according to their effect on acceptance length to reduce the cost of packed drafting. Removing five of 36 layers retains over 99\% of mean acceptance length across three benchmarks with Qwen3-8B as the target. Across seven benchmarks, D3D averages 5.00$\times$ and 5.71$\times$ decoding speedup with Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B targets, compared with 4.03$\times$ and 3.67$\times$ for DFlash. On GSM8K with Qwen3-32B, speedup reaches 7.48$\times$. Code is publicly available at https://github.com/fudan-generative-vision/D3D.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 3: Advances in diffusion language models with a new method (D3D) for asynchronous speculative decoding improving decoding speed for diffusion-based language models.

## 4. [Auditable Unlearning for LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05866) <a id="link4"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.05866
**Authors:** Piyush Tiwary, Anushka Srivastava, Prathosh A. P

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.05866v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Machine unlearning aims to remove a user's data from a trained model after a deletion request. Existing methods are majorly evaluated on three axes - how well they forget, how much capability they retain, and how robust they are to membership inference attacks, but not on whether the provider can offer the user any evidence that the deletion occurred. To fill this gap, we introduce auditability as a fourth axis, defined as the separation between requested (forget set) and retained (retain set) data under a statistic the provider computes. We show that across eleven unlearning methods on TOFU, MUSE and RWKU no method satisfies all four axes, with different methods falling short on different axes. To address this, we propose a two stage method that can be used on top of any unlearning method, in the the first stage we propose ${GapWiden}$ which uses a hinge loss to increase the difference between audit statistics of forget set and retain set. In the second stage we propose ${GapVeil}$, an inference time rejection-sampler which uses the base model as the drafter and re-samples based on the audit statistics of the drafted response. We observe that using our method, auditability and leakage reach oracle parity for every unlearning method, and forget performance converges to the oracle; retain performance improves for nine of eleven methods but remains short of parity. Our results show that evidence of deletion or auditability can be constructed by design, and that treating it as an explicit requirement changes the viability of existing unlearning methods.

**Comment:** This paper studies machine unlearning for LLMs with a novel focus on auditability, proposing methods to provide evidence that data deletion requests were honored. This directly matches criterion 9 on advances in machine unlearning techniques for removing specific knowledge from trained models, with a novel auditability axis.

## 6. [Grounded but Not Immune: Confirmation Bias in Agent-as-a-judge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05662) <a id="link6"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.05662
**Authors:** Drake Benjamin Brown, Khushbu Pahwa, Yubin Ge, Salvatore Romeo, Akhil Agnihotri, Yi Zhang, Xiaofei Ma, Daniele Bonadiman

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.05662v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Evaluating open-ended agent tasks increasingly falls to an Agent-as-a-Judge: an evaluator handed tools and the agent's full trajectory so it can verify a result against the environment. The premise is that a judge that gathers its own evidence should be grounded by it. We show that this grounding does not immunize the judge against confirmation bias: a single false pre-screening note, attributed to an authority and carrying no evidential bearing on the correct verdict, overturns up to 40% of a tool-using judge's correct verdicts across four judges and five datasets, even though the judge collects decisive evidence. Our central contribution is to explain how confirmation bias changes the judge's behavior in a trajectory. We classify changes in judge behavior and attribute changes to one of three failure modes: over-trust (verify, conclude correctly, then defer), hallucination (fabricate support for the flip), and under-investigation (stop at the first corroborating signal). For the two Claude judges, the failure is predominantly over-trust (67-75% of harmful flips), while smaller judges have error modes spread across all three. Lightweight mitigations blunt but never remove the bias.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 4: The paper studies confirmation bias in Agent-as-a-Judge evaluation paradigms for open-ended text generation, fundamentally rethinking evaluation by diagnosing failure modes like over-trust and hallucination in judges.

## Paper selection prompt
 1. New methodological improvements to RLHF or instruction-following which are specific fine-tuning steps that are taken to make language models better at following user instructions across a range of tasks.
    - Relevant: papers that discuss specific methods like RLHF, or instruction-tuning datasets, improving these methods, or analyzing them. Usually these papers will explicitly mention RLHF, instruction-following or instruction-tuning.
    - Not relevant: papers about adaptation to some task. Simply following instructions or inputs are not sufficient.
 2. Shows new powerful test set contamination or membership inference methods for language models. Test set contamination is the phenomenon where a language model observes a benchmark dataset during pretraining.
    - Relevant: test statistics that can detect contamination of benchmarks in language models. statistics that can provide guarantees are more interesting. membership inference methods that are general enough to apply to language models are also relevant.
    - Not relevant: any papers that do not consider language models, or that do not consider test set contamination.
 3. Shows a significant advance in the performance of diffusion language models.
    - Relevant: papers that study language models that are also diffusion models. Continuous diffusions are even more relevant, while discrete diffusions are less so.
    - Not relevant: papers about image diffusions like DALL-E or Stable Diffusion, or papers that do not explicitly mention language models or applications to text.
 4. Describes new paradigms to evaluating open-ended text generation. Evaluating the outputs of language models is hard, especially in open-ended settings like for chatbots.
    - Relevant: papers that fundamentally rethink language model evaluation -- especially by accounting for subjectivity or using adversaries.
    - Not relevant: specific evaluations for specific tasks, identifying new properties or flaws of language models, or simply collecting new data.
 5. Conducts surveys or provides data into real-world usage and safety properties of language models.
    - Relevant: papers that create new datasets or surveys on real-world usage of language models.
    - Not relevant: papers that apply language models to new real-world tasks.
 6. Studies 'scaling laws' in the context of neural networks. Scaling laws refer to the very clear power-law relationship between the size or computational power used to train a model and the performance of that model.
    - Relevant: theoretical or conceptual explanation behind scaling laws for language models.
    - Not relevant: papers that have experiments at different model scales (but do not explicitly fit a scaling law) or papers that mention scaling laws, but the scaling laws are not the central subject of the paper
 7. Presents new methods or improvements for sparse autoencoders in neural network interpretability. Sparse autoencoders are unsupervised learning techniques that learn sparse representations of neural network activations to understand what features networks detect.
    - Relevant: papers that explicitly mention sparse autoencoders, sparse coding, or sparse feature extraction applied to understanding neural networks, language models, or other AI systems.
    - Not relevant: papers about general autoencoders without sparsity constraints, or sparse methods not applied to interpretability.
 8. Demonstrates new mechanistic interpretability techniques or discoveries about how neural networks work internally. Mechanistic interpretability seeks to understand the algorithms and circuits that neural networks learn.
    - Relevant: papers about circuit analysis, feature visualization, activation patching, causal interventions on neural networks, or reverse-engineering specific algorithms learned by models.
    - Not relevant: papers that only do black-box analysis or correlation studies without mechanistic understanding.
 9. Shows advances in machine unlearning techniques for removing specific knowledge or behaviors from trained models.
    - Relevant: papers on forgetting specific data, removing harmful capabilities, or selectively editing model knowledge while preserving other capabilities.
    - Not relevant: papers about general model editing or fine-tuning without specific unlearning focus.
 10. Presents methods for tamper resistance, robustness against adversarial modifications, or secure AI systems.
    - Relevant: papers on preventing model poisoning, backdoor attacks, or making AI systems resistant to tampering or manipulation.
    - Not relevant: papers only about general adversarial robustness without security/tamper resistance focus.
 11. Studies AI alignment techniques, value learning, or methods to ensure AI systems behave according to human values and intentions.
    - Relevant: papers on reward modeling, constitutional AI, value alignment, or techniques to make AI systems more aligned with human preferences.
    - Not relevant: papers focused only on performance improvement without alignment considerations.
 12. Advances in LLM reasoning capabilities, chain-of-thought reasoning, or understanding how language models perform complex reasoning tasks.
    - Relevant: papers on step-by-step reasoning, logical inference in LLMs, reasoning chain analysis, or methods to improve reasoning capabilities.
    - Not relevant: papers about reasoning in non-LLM contexts or that don't focus on reasoning mechanisms.
 13. Presents new methods for AI control, oversight, or monitoring of AI system behavior during deployment.
    - Relevant: papers on AI governance techniques, monitoring systems, control mechanisms, or methods to maintain oversight of deployed AI systems.
    - Not relevant: papers focused only on training-time controls without deployment considerations.
 14. Shows advances in chain-of-thought monitoring, reasoning transparency, or methods to understand AI decision-making processes.
    - Relevant: papers on monitoring reasoning chains, making AI reasoning more transparent, or techniques to track how models arrive at conclusions.
    - Not relevant: papers about general explainability without specific focus on reasoning processes.
 15. Studies ControlArena, Inspect framework, or similar evaluation platforms for AI safety and control.
    - Relevant: papers using or extending these evaluation frameworks, comparative studies using these platforms, or methodological improvements to these tools.
    - Not relevant: papers that mention these tools only in passing without substantial use or analysis.
 16. Advances in latent reasoning, implicit reasoning processes, or understanding hidden computational processes in neural networks.
    - Relevant: papers on uncovering implicit reasoning, analyzing hidden computational steps, or methods to make latent reasoning processes more explicit.
    - Not relevant: papers about explicit reasoning without focus on hidden or latent processes.
 17. Studies data poisoning attacks or defenses in machine learning systems.
    - Relevant: papers on poisoning training data to manipulate model behavior, detecting poisoned data, or defending against data poisoning attacks.
    - Not relevant: papers about general adversarial examples at inference time without training data manipulation.
 18. Advances in data-centric AI approaches that focus on improving data quality, curation, or data-driven methods for ML.
    - Relevant: papers on data quality assessment, data selection, data augmentation strategies, curriculum learning based on data properties, or systematic approaches to dataset creation and curation.
    - Not relevant: papers that simply use standard datasets without focusing on data-centric methodology.
 19. Studies security of LLMs and methods to protect language models from attacks or exploitation.
    - Relevant: papers on prompt injection defenses, jailbreak prevention, securing LLM deployments, protecting against extraction attacks, or hardening LLM systems against adversarial users.
    - Not relevant: papers about using LLMs for security applications, or general robustness without security focus.
