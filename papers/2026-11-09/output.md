## 0. [DUET: Dual-Sparse Inference for Block Diffusion Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09239) <a id="link0"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.09239
**Authors:** Siyuan Yan, Shen Gu, Xiaoxing Ma, Chun Cao, Jingwei Xu

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.09239v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Diffusion language models (DLMs) enable parallel token generation through iterative denoising, yet their inference remains costly due to repeated attention over a growing historical prefix and repeated processing of the entire current generation block. Historical attention becomes increasingly expensive as generation progresses, while all current-block positions are propagated through the Transformer at every denoising step even though only a small fraction are typically resolved at that step. In this paper, we introduce Duet, a training-free dual-sparse inference framework that addresses these challenges through prefix Key-Value (KV) sparsity and early query sparsity, respectively. Specifically, Duet exploits the concentration and cross-step stability of historical attention within each generation block to construct a compact historical KV subset that is reused across denoising steps. For query sparsity, Duet predicts update positions from shallow representations and propagates only the corresponding queries through the suffix Transformer layers, avoiding deep computation at positions unlikely to be updated in the current step. Experiments show that Duet maintains competitive accuracy while achieving 1.34$\times$/2.83$\times$ end-to-end/per-step speedups on LLaDA2.1-mini and 2.41$\times$/4.72$\times$ on SDAR-8B-Chat-b32 at a 32K context length.

**Comment:** This paper presents DUET, a dual-sparse inference method for diffusion language models that significantly speeds up inference while maintaining accuracy. It directly advances diffusion language models (criterion 3) by addressing computational efficiency in continuous diffusion LM inference.

## 1. [A Tight Hierarchy for Chain of Thought](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09759) <a id="link1"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.09759
**Authors:** Thanh Le, A. Pavan, N. V. Vinodchandran

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.09759v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for extending the computational capabilities of transformer-based models. A growing body of recent work has begun to uncover the fine-grained computational power of transformers augmented with CoT, as well as their connections to classical complexity theory.   In this work, we advance this line of research by establishing the first hierarchy theorem for CoT-based computation. Our main result shows that for any reasonable resource bound $r(n) \geq n$ and any $\varepsilon > 0$, \[ \mathrm{CoT}(r(n)) \subsetneq \mathrm{CoT}(r(n)^{1+\varepsilon}), \] where $\mathrm{CoT}(r(n))$ denotes the class of decision problems solvable by transformers using $O(r(n))$ CoT reasoning steps on inputs of length $n$. Our result shows that increasing the number of CoT reasoning steps provably yields strictly greater computational power, establishing CoT steps as a fundamental resource in transformer-based computation.   Unlike classical hierarchy theorems, which are typically proved via diagonalization, our approach leverages known coarse-grained simulations between CoT-augmented transformers and Turing machines in both directions. Building on these connections, we adapt the classical padding technique from complexity theory to this setting to obtain a tight hierarchy. A key technical contribution of our work is the implementation of padding within the transformer-based CoT model.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 12: Provides a theoretical hierarchy theorem for chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning steps in transformer-based models, showing that more CoT steps strictly increase computational power. This is a significant theoretical advance in understanding LLM reasoning capabilities.

## 2. [Diffusion-free Score Matching for Exact Conditional Sampling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09758) <a id="link2"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.09758
**Authors:** Thomas Wedenig, Robert Peharz

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.09758v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: The success of modern score-based generative models is largely driven by diffusion-based training, which learns a collection of time-indexed score fields corresponding to progressively noisier versions of the data distribution. While such models achieve impressive sample quality, leveraging them for sound probabilistic inference---particularly sampling from arbitrary conditionals of the model---remains challenging. This difficulty arises because conditioning information is only available for clean data and not for the higher noise levels required to generate exact conditional samples. In this paper, we introduce DIffusion-free SCOre matching (DISCO), a principled approach that sidesteps the need for time-dependent score fields altogether. Our method is based on an objective that, while reminiscent of diffusion-based training, estimates only a single score of the (slightly perturbed) data distribution, for which the exact conditional score is readily obtained. In experiments, DISCO is competitive with state-of-the-art diffusion models in terms of sample quality. More importantly, DISCO yields a more faithful representation of the underlying data distribution and---crucially---enables accurate sampling from arbitrary conditionals, outperforming standard heuristic samplers. This capability opens the door to principled and flexible probabilistic reasoning with score-based models.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 3: Presents a new method (DISCO) for diffusion language models that avoids time-dependent score fields and enables exact conditional sampling, improving probabilistic inference with score-based generative models.

## 3. [Aspect-wise Pairwise Tournaments for Test-Time Selection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08640) <a id="link3"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.08640
**Authors:** Hannah Guan, Yilun Du

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.08640v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Scaling test-time compute has proven highly effective at improving LLM reasoning. A popular method is best-of-n sampling, where a model generates many candidate solutions and one is selected. This motivates the question: how can we select the best candidate? When humans make choices, we find it more natural to compare two options than judge each in isolation. Additionally, when the options become complex, we like to judge the options one aspect at a time, such as comparing two job candidates on experience, communication, etc. In the test-time setting, candidate solutions should be judged in the same way. Here we introduce Aspect-wise Pairwise Tournaments (APT), which verifies by comparison and compares by aspect. Each matchup of a single elimination tournament is decided by a vote over multiple off-the-shelf aspect verifiers, each expressing a pairwise preference along its own aspect. Across five domains and three generator LLMs, APT improves over binary multi-aspect approval, self-consistency and reward model verification, with the largest gains on open-ended tasks. We find that both components matter; neither binary approval over the same aspects nor a generic pairwise judge at a matched budget matches APT. Because aspects are specified in natural language, APT also lets users steer selection toward properties they care about, better aligning with user preferences without needing to regenerate solutions. No component is trained, so APT transfers to new domains by simply rewriting prompts.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 4: Proposes Aspect-wise Pairwise Tournaments (APT), a new paradigm for evaluating open-ended LLM outputs by pairwise comparison across multiple aspects, improving over existing verification methods and allowing user steering without training.

## 4. [Alignment by Confident Debate: Preference Learning from Multi-Agent Debate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09568) <a id="link4"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.09568
**Authors:** Nikhil Verma, Dingxian Wang, Mohammad Reza Rezaei, Andrew Rabinovich, Zhao Chen

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.09568v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Post-training language models for reasoning typically rely on gold answers, task-specific verifiers, or learned reward models, limiting scalability when such signals are scarce. Multi-agent debate offers a self-supervised alternative, but converting debate into reliable training supervision remains challenging: majority voting weights agreeing responses equally, lacks a principled tie-breaking rule, and is ill-defined for open-ended tasks without a canonical answer; likelihood-based scoring can conflate fluency with correctness; and homogeneous committees may reinforce correlated errors. We introduce Alignment by Confident Debate (AbCD), which converts debate traces into preference data without gold answer labels. A sampling-diverse committee debates each problem, after which final answers are clustered by task-level semantic equivalence. We use verifiable tasks (mathematical reasoning, multiple-choice quantitative QA, and executable code-generation) as a controlled test bed: their answer keys or executable checkers let us evaluate the quality of debate-derived supervision against ground truth, even though the oracle itself never uses gold labels. Post-training on the resulting preferences outperforms inference-only ensembling and debate, as well as SFT on the same traces, across five model families spanning 1B to 8B parameters and four datasets, improving consensus accuracy, with a maximum gain of $21.5_{pp}$. Ablations show that the approach transfers to a larger model and to code generation, while also revealing limitations of heterogeneous committees and agreement-based weighting. Overall, the results suggest that debate traces can yield useful preference supervision when answer equivalence is combined with confidence-weighted disagreement.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 1: Proposes a new method for preference learning from multi-agent debate traces to improve instruction-following and alignment without gold labels, which is a novel fine-tuning approach related to RLHF and instruction-following improvements.

## 5. [RuACT: Recovering Inactive Rubrics as Adaptive Critics and Teachers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09597) <a id="link5"></a>
**ArXiv ID:** 2611.09597
**Authors:** Yijun Li, Zhengxi Lu, Zhipiao Liu, Jinzheng Yu, Xutao Weng, Jian Shao, Yongliang Shen

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.09597v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Rubric-based reinforcement learning (RL) extends RL with verifiable rewards to open-ended tasks by scoring each response against query-specific rubrics. Under group-relative optimization, however, a rubric that every sampled response fails (unreachable) or passes (saturated) gives all responses the same outcome and no learning signal; in Qwen3-4B medical training, 60.88% of query-rubric observations are inactive. RuACT outperforms GRPO, rubric reweighting, and rubric-guided exploration on all four medical and scientific benchmarks with Qwen3-4B and achieves the best average with Qwen3-1.7B. On initially unreachable rubrics, unguided recovery rises from 35.02% to 42.86%, while child refinement improves reward-quality alignment on independent rollouts from 57.74% to 60.77%. Code is available at https://github.com/ZJU-REAL/RuACT.

## 8. [SIFR: Stable Signed Flow Reinforcement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09175) <a id="link8"></a>

**Abstract:** arXiv:2611.09175v1 Announce Type: new  Abstract: Reward-based fine-tuning of flow models requires effectively promoting high-reward and suppressing low-reward samples, while maintaining training stability over many rounds of iterative updates. ## Paper selection prompt
 1. New methodological improvements to RLHF or instruction-following which are specific fine-tuning steps that are taken to make language models better at following user instructions across a range of tasks.
    - Relevant: papers that discuss specific methods like RLHF, or instruction-tuning datasets, improving these methods, or analyzing them. Usually these papers will explicitly mention RLHF, instruction-following or instruction-tuning.
    - Not relevant: papers about adaptation to some task. Simply following instructions or inputs are not sufficient.
 2. Shows new powerful test set contamination or membership inference methods for language models. Test set contamination is the phenomenon where a language model observes a benchmark dataset during pretraining.
    - Relevant: test statistics that can detect contamination of benchmarks in language models. statistics that can provide guarantees are more interesting. membership inference methods that are general enough to apply to language models are also relevant.
    - Not relevant: any papers that do not consider language models, or that do not consider test set contamination.
 3. Shows a significant advance in the performance of diffusion language models.
    - Relevant: papers that study language models that are also diffusion models. Continuous diffusions are even more relevant, while discrete diffusions are less so.
    - Not relevant: papers about image diffusions like DALL-E or Stable Diffusion, or papers that do not explicitly mention language models or applications to text.
 4. Describes new paradigms to evaluating open-ended text generation. Evaluating the outputs of language models is hard, especially in open-ended settings like for chatbots.
    - Relevant: papers that fundamentally rethink language model evaluation -- especially by accounting for subjectivity or using adversaries.
    - Not relevant: specific evaluations for specific tasks, identifying new properties or flaws of language models, or simply collecting new data.
 5. Conducts surveys or provides data into real-world usage and safety properties of language models.
    - Relevant: papers that create new datasets or surveys on real-world usage of language models.
    - Not relevant: papers that apply language models to new real-world tasks.
 6. Studies 'scaling laws' in the context of neural networks. Scaling laws refer to the very clear power-law relationship between the size or computational power used to train a model and the performance of that model.
    - Relevant: theoretical or conceptual explanation behind scaling laws for language models.
    - Not relevant: papers that have experiments at different model scales (but do not explicitly fit a scaling law) or papers that mention scaling laws, but the scaling laws are not the central subject of the paper
 7. Presents new methods or improvements for sparse autoencoders in neural network interpretability. Sparse autoencoders are unsupervised learning techniques that learn sparse representations of neural network activations to understand what features networks detect.
    - Relevant: papers that explicitly mention sparse autoencoders, sparse coding, or sparse feature extraction applied to understanding neural networks, language models, or other AI systems.
    - Not relevant: papers about general autoencoders without sparsity constraints, or sparse methods not applied to interpretability.
 8. Demonstrates new mechanistic interpretability techniques or discoveries about how neural networks work internally. Mechanistic interpretability seeks to understand the algorithms and circuits that neural networks learn.
    - Relevant: papers about circuit analysis, feature visualization, activation patching, causal interventions on neural networks, or reverse-engineering specific algorithms learned by models.
    - Not relevant: papers that only do black-box analysis or correlation studies without mechanistic understanding.
 9. Shows advances in machine unlearning techniques for removing specific knowledge or behaviors from trained models.
    - Relevant: papers on forgetting specific data, removing harmful capabilities, or selectively editing model knowledge while preserving other capabilities.
    - Not relevant: papers about general model editing or fine-tuning without specific unlearning focus.
 10. Presents methods for tamper resistance, robustness against adversarial modifications, or secure AI systems.
    - Relevant: papers on preventing model poisoning, backdoor attacks, or making AI systems resistant to tampering or manipulation.
    - Not relevant: papers only about general adversarial robustness without security/tamper resistance focus.
 11. Studies AI alignment techniques, value learning, or methods to ensure AI systems behave according to human values and intentions.
    - Relevant: papers on reward modeling, constitutional AI, value alignment, or techniques to make AI systems more aligned with human preferences.
    - Not relevant: papers focused only on performance improvement without alignment considerations.
 12. Advances in LLM reasoning capabilities, chain-of-thought reasoning, or understanding how language models perform complex reasoning tasks.
    - Relevant: papers on step-by-step reasoning, logical inference in LLMs, reasoning chain analysis, or methods to improve reasoning capabilities.
    - Not relevant: papers about reasoning in non-LLM contexts or that don't focus on reasoning mechanisms.
 13. Presents new methods for AI control, oversight, or monitoring of AI system behavior during deployment.
    - Relevant: papers on AI governance techniques, monitoring systems, control mechanisms, or methods to maintain oversight of deployed AI systems.
    - Not relevant: papers focused only on training-time controls without deployment considerations.
 14. Shows advances in chain-of-thought monitoring, reasoning transparency, or methods to understand AI decision-making processes.
    - Relevant: papers on monitoring reasoning chains, making AI reasoning more transparent, or techniques to track how models arrive at conclusions.
    - Not relevant: papers about general explainability without specific focus on reasoning processes.
 15. Studies ControlArena, Inspect framework, or similar evaluation platforms for AI safety and control.
    - Relevant: papers using or extending these evaluation frameworks, comparative studies using these platforms, or methodological improvements to these tools.
    - Not relevant: papers that mention these tools only in passing without substantial use or analysis.
 16. Advances in latent reasoning, implicit reasoning processes, or understanding hidden computational processes in neural networks.
    - Relevant: papers on uncovering implicit reasoning, analyzing hidden computational steps, or methods to make latent reasoning processes more explicit.
    - Not relevant: papers about explicit reasoning without focus on hidden or latent processes.
 17. Studies data poisoning attacks or defenses in machine learning systems.
    - Relevant: papers on poisoning training data to manipulate model behavior, detecting poisoned data, or defending against data poisoning attacks.
    - Not relevant: papers about general adversarial examples at inference time without training data manipulation.
 18. Advances in data-centric AI approaches that focus on improving data quality, curation, or data-driven methods for ML.
    - Relevant: papers on data quality assessment, data selection, data augmentation strategies, curriculum learning based on data properties, or systematic approaches to dataset creation and curation.
    - Not relevant: papers that simply use standard datasets without focusing on data-centric methodology.
 19. Studies security of LLMs and methods to protect language models from attacks or exploitation.
    - Relevant: papers on prompt injection defenses, jailbreak prevention, securing LLM deployments, protecting against extraction attacks, or hardening LLM systems against adversarial users.
    - Not relevant: papers about using LLMs for security applications, or general robustness without security focus.
